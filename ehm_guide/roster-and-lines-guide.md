# Eastside Hockey Manager – Roster Construction and Line Management Guide

This guide consolidates community knowledge on building a competitive roster, constructing effective forward lines and defensive pairings, using individual and unit tactics, and troubleshooting common performance problems. It draws primarily on discussion from 2008 through 2020, covering both EHM 2007 and EHM 1. Where differences between the two versions are noted they are flagged; in most respects the principles described here apply equally to both.

## Roster Building Principles

The most consistent piece of roster-building advice in the community is to identify your **core** early and build outward from it. The core of a competitive NHL team consists of:

- A top goaltender
- At least three high-quality defencemen
- Two strong centres for the top six forwards
- Two strong wingers for the top six forwards
- Four pacy, well-rounded wingers to fill the middle six
- Reliable depth centres for the bottom six

Everything else is complementary. Filling out the rest of the roster with players you can move — depth forwards on two-way deals, interchangeable third-pairing defencemen — gives you flexibility to build through trades without sacrificing the core.

A useful mental model for roster construction:

**Fix your offence first.** The game rewards goal-scoring heavily in terms of average ratings and morale, and a team that scores freely will often win despite average defensive play. This does not mean defence is unimportant, but when forced to choose between improving a goal-scorer and improving a stay-at-home defenceman, experienced community members tend to prioritise the scorer. The goaltender is the exception and should always be a priority.

**Avoid no-trade clauses wherever possible.** They remove flexibility when cap management requires moving pieces. Paying slightly less on a tradeable contract is nearly always preferable.

**Sign "too many" useful players at moderate salaries.** A roster slightly overstocked with competent second and third-tier players on two-way deals is easier to manage than a lean roster with no depth. Extras can be bundled into trades or moved at the deadline.

**Avoid overloading the top of the cap.** Multiple community saves report that very top-heavy salary structures cause downstream problems — difficulty re-signing prospects, mid-season cap crunches, and reduced flexibility at the trade deadline. The standard advice is to keep at least 2–3 players in each tier of the lineup on team-friendly deals.

**Checking lines and pure physical lines are generally not recommended.** The community consensus, developed over many years, is that dedicated "checking lines" populated entirely by defensive specialists without offensive skill tend to underperform in terms of average ratings and +/−. The game rewards points and goal contributions heavily, and a line that scores nothing but prevents goals will still accumulate poor morale if the players on it produce poorly. The better approach is to build third and fourth lines with players who are defensively responsible *and* capable of contributing offensively — versatile two-way forwards rather than pure checkers.

## Forward Line Construction

### General Principles

Lines work best when the players on them **complement rather than duplicate each other's skills**. Putting a purely defensive centre alongside a purely offensive winger can underperform relative to a line where all three players have compatible skill profiles. A defensive-style centre combined with a sniper on the wing, for example, tends to produce below-expectation results for the sniper because the centre will not generate the creative passing that a sniper needs. As a practical rule: match tactics to your linemates' attributes, not to individual player ideals in isolation.

The right-hand shot question comes up regularly. Having at least one right-handed shot on each forward line is worth targeting, both for power-play unit balance and because natural side wingers tend to play better on their natural side. That said, accomplished (as opposed to natural) positional players on the wrong side can still perform well; this is more a quality-of-life optimisation than a hard requirement.

### The Top Six

The top two lines should be built primarily for offence. The first line needs your two best wingers and your best playmaking or scoring centre. The second line can often carry a more defensively versatile centre but should still be offensively minded — a second line that scores nothing creates morale and chemistry problems quickly.

Avoid running five or six franchise-level forwards in your top six if the cap forces your bottom six to be made up entirely of minimum-salary fringe players. The middle tier of the roster (lines three and four) matters for overall team health even if individual games are mostly decided by the top six.

Young prospects placed in the top six — particularly as the only top-six option available — can perform well, but should be given ice time that matches their current ability level rather than being dropped to fourth-line checking minutes, which stunts development and hurts morale.

### The Bottom Six

> ⚠️ There is community disagreement on this point. The older view favoured dedicated checking lines with specialist defensive forwards; the more widely held view from later community discussion is that pure checking lines underperform in the game and that bottom-six forwards need offensive capability to maintain ratings and morale.

Third and fourth lines in EHM perform best when composed of players who can contribute points while also playing responsibly. An ideal third-line winger or centre is a **two-way forward** — someone whose attributes include reasonable offensive production (stickhandling, passing, shooting) alongside solid defensive skills (pokecheck, positioning, work rate). Pure checkers whose attributes are entirely defensive tend to produce poor average ratings because the game rewards scoring contributions heavily.

A **fourth-line development approach** works well: use one veteran depth player (often available cheaply) alongside two young prospects who need ice time but are not yet ready for the top six. This keeps costs low, develops young players, and the veteran provides stability.

### The Third-Line Centre Problem

One of the most frequently discussed problems in the community is the difficulty of finding an effective **third-line centre**. The issue is structural: third-line centres receive limited ice time, often get matched against the opponent's top line (especially with line matching enabled), and accumulate poor +/− and rating numbers as a result. This creates a negative morale spiral that is very hard to break once it starts.

Practical guidance from the community:

- Setting the third line to a fully defensive tactical profile makes the rating and morale problem *worse*, not better, because the centre produces even fewer points and morale suffers.
- Focusing the third line **offensively** — accepting some defensive vulnerability in exchange for point production — tends to produce better average ratings and keeps morale manageable.
- Giving the third-line centre occasional power-play duty provides a morale boost and accumulates points.
- A veteran centre with high Determination and Teamwork handles the limited role better than a talented young player who expects more ice time.
- Disabling line matching removes the scenario where your third line is repeatedly deployed against the opponent's best forwards.
- The fourth-line centre is a different problem and tends to be easier to manage: a cheap, experienced veteran who understands a limited role will generally cope without morale issues and can contribute 20–30 points from minimal ice time.

## Defensive Pairing Construction

### Pairing Philosophy

The most widely recommended defensive pairing structure is to put one offensive defenceman and one defensive defenceman on each pair. Playing two offensive defencemen together, except on the power play, frequently leads to goals against. The common pattern used by experienced community members:

- **Pair 1:** Best overall offensive defenceman paired with a reliable stay-at-home partner
- **Pair 2:** Balanced pairing with both players capable of some offensive contribution and solid defensive play
- **Pair 3:** Two defensive-minded defencemen, with the flexibility to include a young prospect learning the NHL level

Two offensive defencemen should only ever be paired together on the **power play** where the defensive liability is tolerable and the offensive upside is worth it.

When a team's defensive issues stem from an all-offence blue line (too many puck-movers, no stay-at-home types), the solution is to acquire at least two genuine defensive defencemen regardless of the offensive cost. A defence corps skewed entirely towards offence will bleed goals even with a capable goaltender.

### Attributes for Defencemen

**Defensive defencemen** should prioritise: Positioning, Pokecheck, Checking, Anticipation, Bravery, and physical attributes (Strength, Speed, Stamina). Hitting is a bonus.

**Offensive defencemen** should additionally prioritise: Passing, Creativity, Shooting (Wristshot or Slapshot depending on role), and the ability to join the rush (`Join Rush` set in personal tactics). Offensive defencemen with very high technical attributes but poor Positioning and Anticipation can produce poor average ratings at even strength despite impressive point totals; pairing them with a defensive-minded partner mitigates this.

### Getting the Most from Offensive Defencemen

Offensive defencemen (in the Rafalski, Bouwmeester, Visnovsky mould) require specific handling to avoid being a defensive liability. Community guidance:

- Set their **mentality to Normal or Defensive** at even strength. Setting them to Offensive at even strength leads to goals against even when paired with a defensive partner.
- Use them aggressively on the **power play** (Offensive mentality is appropriate there).
- Enable **Join Rush** and **Carry Puck** in personal tactics if their Deking and Stickhandling are high enough to support it.
- Pair with a player who stays back reliably. The combination of one attacker and one homebody produces better outcomes than two attackers.
- Do not set more than one defenceman per pair to an aggressive attacking approach at even strength.

## Penalty Kill

The most important attribute for a **penalty-killing defenceman** is **Positioning**, followed by Pokecheck, Anticipation, Checking, and Teamwork. The recommended PK pairing selection is simply to take the four defencemen with the highest Positioning ratings, unless one of the four has a glaring weakness in another critical area (very poor skating or Anticipation). The standard pairing structure is first-pair defencemen together and second-pair defencemen together (1-4 and 2-3 in depth-chart terms).

For **penalty-killing forwards**, look for high Pokecheck, Positioning, Work Rate, Anticipation, and Teamwork. Stamina is important because PK forwards play at a high intensity for short shifts.

A newly arrived European player may need a season to adjust before being placed on the penalty kill. Their physical condition and familiarity with the team's system matter; some community members prefer to wait one season before assigning PK duties to international arrivals.

## Power Play

Power play units should be constructed around **one or two defencemen on the points** who have strong passing and shooting. A defenceman with an exceptional Slapshot (17+) on the point can make a significant difference to PP production even from the third pairing at even strength.

The **diamond formation** (one point man, two flanks, one net-front, one high forward) is mentioned as a commonly used configuration. It works best when the net-front player is large and strong enough to maintain position (high Strength and Balance) and the point man has both a strong shot and good passing.

Having **at least one right-handed shot on each PP unit** enables shots from the natural side and makes it harder for penalty-killing forwards to predict puck movement. A PP unit with all left-handed shots is predictable and easier to defend.

The second PP unit benefits from including developing young players who need offensive confidence and points to maintain morale.

## Tactics: Individual and Unit Settings

### The Case for Personal Tactics

The most detailed tactical approach used by experienced community members is to set **personal tactics for every player**, tuned to their individual attributes and role. This means:

- A checking forward's personal tactics emphasise positioning, backchecking, and gap control
- A sniper's personal tactics emphasise offensive mentality, creative passing, and high shooting frequency
- A physical enforcer's tactics set hitting to Hard and aggressiveness to Aggressive
- A two-way centre uses balanced settings across the board

The argument for this approach is that every player on the roster is then playing to their strengths, and line chemistry is achieved by combining players whose individual settings are compatible rather than by setting identical unit tactics. However, this approach requires significant investment of time and experimentation.

### Unit Tactics as the Alternative

The simpler approach is to use **unit tactics** (one set of settings per line) with minimal individual adjustments for players who significantly differ from the line's style. For example, a second line set to "speedy offensive" can have one winger's hitting turned up slightly if that player is a power forward, without redoing the entire unit.

The key warning with unit tactics: **avoid extreme settings (Very Hard or Very Offensive) on the first two or three lines during regular play**. Setting everything to maximum intensity tires players quickly and, paradoxically, can lower ratings because tired players make more mistakes. Extreme settings are appropriate for the **power play and penalty kill**, where shifts are shorter and maximum intensity is warranted. For even-strength play, moderate settings produce more sustainable results.

As a general principle: if the tactical approach is working, leave it alone. Constant tinkering with line combinations and tactics — more than every five to six games without a specific reason — tends to disrupt chemistry and lower overall performance.

### Matching Tactics to Player Type

A frequent cause of underperformance is mismatching tactical settings with a player's natural game. Examples from community discussion:

- A defensive centre placed on an offensively-set line will underperform because he is being asked to play in a way that does not suit his attributes.
- An offensive winger given maximally defensive tactical settings will produce fewer points than the same player in balanced settings — even on a checking line, a small offensive role produces better output.
- Players asked to play significantly above their natural ability level (for example, a third-line player deployed as a first-liner for extended stretches) will accumulate morale problems and declining ratings.

## Tactical Rotation and Opponent Countering

### How Counter-Tactics Work

Opponents in EHM will begin to counter a tactic that has been used repeatedly without change. The precise mechanism is not documented in the game, but community experience indicates that AI teams become progressively better at defending against a tactic the more games they have seen it — both within a season and particularly within a playoff series.

> ⚠️ There is community disagreement on this point. Some experienced players rotate tactics routinely (approximately every five or six games or after any significant loss); others rarely change their tactics, relying instead on individually tuned personal tactics which they argue produce enough variation by themselves to prevent effective counter-tactics. Both approaches have produced successful results in reported saves.

The consensus guidance for **playoff series** is clearer: in a seven-game series, rotating between two or three variations of the same overall approach (primarily varying breakout and neutral zone schemes) prevents the opponent from fully adapting. The variations do not need to be dramatic — small changes to breakout patterns, gap control settings, or forward mentality are sufficient. "Completely different system" changes are not recommended or necessary.

### Practical Rotation

A workable approach for rotation is to maintain **two or three saved tactic variations** that share the same basic structure but differ in one or two settings (mentality, passing style, or backchecking intensity, for example). Alternate between them every five to six games during the regular season and every other game during a playoff series.

This approach is widely adopted and aligns with the real-life pattern of coaches adjusting game-to-game without changing the fundamental system.

### Playoff Tactics

When holding a lead late in a playoff game, switching to a more defensive line configuration — moving checked-out third-liners to the first and second lines to keep the top players fresh — can help protect the lead without conceding excessive chances. Distributing ice time more evenly across the lineup during a long series keeps star players from accumulating fatigue that would harm their playoff performance.

## Goaltender Management

### Workload Rotation

The consistent community recommendation is to start the backup goaltender **approximately 10–15 games per season** (roughly every 10–12 games for the starter). A starter who plays 70–75 games per season is manageable; a starter playing every game may start to show fatigue effects or, in some saves, reduced consistency from overwork.

Backup goalies who sit behind a dominant starter without getting games will eventually become unhappy and seek a trade. Giving the backup a regular rotation of starts avoids this problem and maintains depth cover.

If a starting goaltender's performance drops significantly (save percentage falling from normal levels, for example), two explanations are worth checking before making a transaction: whether the goalie is receiving adequate rest (introducing more backup starts); and whether the tactic counter problem has left the team conceding more shots. A decline in goalie performance is sometimes a symptom of a tactical issue rather than a player problem.

### Goalie Tactics

Community guidance on goalie tactical settings is minimal but consistent: **set the goaltender to stay in the crease** unless his Stickhandling attribute is high enough to justify involvement in puck play. Most goalies are best played in a passive, position-focused style with safe passing. Aggressive goalie settings on a keeper with poor stick skills produce unnecessary turnovers.

## Attributes, Ratings, and Player Performance

### Attributes Are Not Everything

One of the most consistently stated principles in the community is that **visible attributes are not a reliable predictor of in-game performance**, particularly for individual players. Multiple examples from community discussion:

- A defenceman with Checking 18, Positioning 20, and Pokecheck 18 can post a 5–6 average rating and a negative +/−, while a less-attributed defenceman finds his role and delivers consistent 7s.
- A forward scouted as a franchise player with technical attributes of 16–19 across the board can fail to exceed five goals in a full season.
- Conversely, players whose scouts dismiss as third-liners or worse regularly develop into reliable second-liners or better.

The reasons for this are not fully documented, but the community's practical guidance is: **let performance determine playing time, not attributes**. If a player with lesser attributes is consistently rating higher than a theoretically superior alternative, give the performer the ice time and reduce the underperformer's role. Players who cannot maintain a 7.0 average rating generally do not belong in a role that demands it, regardless of what their attribute page shows.

**Hidden attributes** (potential ability, consistency, mental characteristics not visible without the editor) play a significant role in explaining the gap between attribute profiles and actual performance. Players with high Flair but low Consistency will produce spectacular games and poor games in roughly equal measure. Players with poor hidden Consistency are the most likely explanation for "franchise-quality" players who cannot produce reliably.

### Using Average Ratings as a Benchmark

Practical rating benchmarks used by experienced community members:

| Player role | Minimum acceptable average rating |
|---|---|
| First-line forward | 7.5–8.0 |
| Second-line forward | 7.0–7.5 |
| Third/fourth-line forward | 6.5–7.0 |
| Top-pairing defenceman | 7.5+ |
| Third-pairing defenceman | 6.5–7.0 |
| Starting goaltender | 8.0+ |
| Backup goaltender | 7.5+ |

Players who consistently fall below these benchmarks in their assigned role should be moved to a lower role, given a tactical adjustment, or replaced. Persisting with an underperforming player in the hope that attributes will eventually translate into results tends to prolong poor team performance and create morale issues.

A player who suddenly produces poor ratings after previously performing well may be experiencing a morale issue, tactical counter effects, or fatigue. Resting them for a practice session, providing a tactical change, or shifting them to a slightly different line combination is worth trying before concluding that the player has fundamentally declined.

### Shutout Ratings

A goaltender who records a shutout does not automatically receive a 10 rating. The rating reflects the difficulty of the save work performed: a 13-save shutout against low-quality shots earns a modest rating, while a 35-save shutout including multiple breakaways and high-danger chances earns a high one. This is working as intended.

## Troubleshooting Common Problems

### Stars Not Performing to Expectations

If first-line or franchise players are producing 7–8 average ratings when higher is expected:

- Check whether their **individual tactics match their attributes**. A player set to maximally defensive tactics with creative offensive attributes will underperform.
- Check whether their **linemates are compatible**. A finesse centre on a line with two grinding power forwards, or a passer without a shooter to target, will not produce.
- Check whether **extreme tactical settings** (Very Hard backchecking, Very Heavy puck pressure) are being applied to all lines — these settings tire players and reduce performance.
- Check whether a tactical **counter** has developed: if the same tactics have been running without change for 15–20 games, rotating to a variation can restore performance.
- Check **stamina and condition**. A player noted as "in need of rest" in news items is carrying fatigue that affects performance even when their listed stamina reads high.

### Defence Allowing Too Many Goals

If the team is giving up significantly more goals than expected:

- The most common structural cause is a **blue line skewed entirely towards offence** with no genuine stay-at-home defencemen. Having two offensive defencemen on each pairing (except the power play) is the primary culprit. Acquiring at least two defensive-minded defencemen and building one defensive pairing per pair fixes this.
- Check that **PK personnel** are selected for Positioning first, not reputation or overall attributes.
- Check that individual and unit tactical settings are not set to maximally offensive at even strength.
- Ensure the starting goaltender is getting **adequate rest** — a tired goaltender's performance deteriorates in ways that resemble a defensive breakdown.

### Bottom Six Consistently Underperforming

If the third and fourth lines are producing consistently poor ratings and large negative +/−:

- Check that these lines are not being matched against the opponent's top line constantly. Disabling line matching, or setting the third line to match a different opponent line, reduces this exposure.
- Consider whether the **players on these lines are simply not good enough** for NHL duty. The community is direct on this: there is no tactical fix for players who lack the ability for their role. The correct solution is replacement, not tactical adjustment.
- Pure checking forwards (very high defensive attributes, very low offensive attributes) tend to produce poor ratings in the game regardless of tactics. Replacing them with two-way forwards who can contribute some offence while playing responsibly usually produces better results.
- Third-line centres specifically benefit from **a focus on offence**, not defence, even on a line intended for defensive duty. See the [third-line centre section](#the-third-line-centre-problem) above.

### Player Consistently Underperforming Despite Good Attributes

If a player with strong visible attributes is consistently rating 1–2 points below expectation:

- First, ensure their tactical settings match their playing style. A mismatch between tactics and attributes is the most common correctable cause.
- Give them **time to adjust** after a trade or signing. Players new to a team often take 10–15 games to settle; 10 games is generally not enough to judge a new acquisition.
- If no improvement after adjustment and settling time, accept that the player may have **poor hidden attributes** (low Consistency, low hidden potential) that explain the gap between their attribute profile and their performance. Bench them, reduce their role, or trade them.
- The hard truth stated by multiple community members: some players with good attribute pages simply do not perform well in EHM, and no tactic or line combination will change that. When performance does not follow attributes after exhausting reasonable adjustments, the right response is to move on.

### Roster Lines Resetting After Games

The game occasionally resets line assignments to defaults after certain events. The fix is to go to **General Manager Options** and enable both "Use the current team selection when possible" and "Use current lines when possible." This prevents the game from overriding manually set lines.
