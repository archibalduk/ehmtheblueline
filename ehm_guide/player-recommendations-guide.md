## General Signing and Acquisition Advice

### Finding Free Agents and UFAs

The Player Search screen contains a free agent filter under Contract → UFA. This produces a list of every unrestricted free agent in the game. Sorting by reputation gives a rough "franchise player to depth player" ordering, and additional attribute filters can be used to narrow by position or skill. The list can be very long on July 1, so it is worth scrolling past the high-reputation names to find solid role players.

Checking contract expiry dates team by team before July 1 is the most reliable way to identify upcoming UFAs early. Once a player hits the free agent market there is no guarantee they will sign with your club, particularly if your team has a low reputation.

For restricted free agents (RFAs), the recommended approach is to make a qualifying offer at the start of the season and then wait. Many RFAs who initially refuse will come back to reasonable terms later in the offseason or at training camp. Letting a player become unhappy is not always necessary — simply holding their rights and inviting them to camp often produces a fair deal. European players in particular may request trades or show unhappiness during the playoffs if their contract situation is unresolved; this is more common with European players than North American ones.

When a player demands a salary that seems too high, making a qualifying offer and letting them sit on the RFA market works well. Other teams rarely sign them on offer sheets, and after a period without a deal their demands tend to come down significantly.

Contract length matters. Older players often prefer longer deals that secure their future, while developing players may resist long contracts if they expect their value to rise sharply. Offering slightly more money and an extra year on the deal is a useful tactic for locking up a promising young player before they price themselves out of reach.

Players can only extend their current contract once they are in its final year.

### Trading and Waivers

The AI trading system values picks highly — sometimes more than their realistic worth. Offering multiple lower-round picks for a good player is often more effective than offering a single higher pick. Teams on the block list are willing to deal; checking the trade centre for players listed as available is the most efficient route to finding deals. Teams out of playoff contention near the trade deadline are also more open to moving players for picks and prospects.

When a player is "set available for financial reasons," this visibly lowers their trade value because other GMs know you need to move them. It is better to approach target teams directly without flagging the player as available.

Waiver claims are a productive source of depth players. Teams regularly pass players through waivers to make roster room or clear salary. Checking the waiver wire for useful role players and defensive specialists is worth doing routinely. Players claimed on waivers count against the cap immediately.

### Signing Overseas Players

European players playing in strong leagues (KHL, SHL, DEL, Liiga) can be signed during the international transfer window, which opens in the summer. They cannot join an NHL roster mid-season outside of that window unless they are already under an NHL contract. European players signed to NHL contracts can be assigned to an AHL affiliate at any time.

Players under contract with European clubs who have an NHL release clause can be signed directly. Transfer fees apply — typically around $250,000 for a player signed away mid-contract.

Russian players in particular often prefer to stay in the KHL. Offering more money or a more prominent role can overcome this, but some players will simply never agree to come over.

## Drafting Advice

### What to Look for in the Draft

The most consistent guidance across many years of community experience is to prioritise **Determination**, **Work Rate**, and **Consistency** when evaluating prospects. These mental attributes are better predictors of whether a player will reach their potential than raw technical attributes at the time of drafting. A player with high technical attributes but low Determination and Work Rate frequently underperforms or stagnates; a player with average attributes but strong mental characteristics may develop well beyond expectations.

Scout report language is also a useful guide. Players described as "Determined," "Ambitious," or of "Strong Character" tend to develop more reliably. "Spineless" is the most damaging mental characteristic — it significantly hampers a player's ability to perform in difficult situations and is generally worth avoiding.

The **Potential Ability** (PA) hidden stat drives development ceiling, but players whose PA is close to their current ability will not improve much further. The best prospects have high PA combined with strong Determination and Work Rate, giving them both the ceiling and the work ethic to reach it.

In practical terms, looking at the number of lines in a head scout's report is a useful shorthand for overall quality. Better prospects receive more detailed reviews. Combining this with visible mental attributes and average ratings in their current league produces a reasonable ranking.

When sending scouts to the draft, the consensus recommendation is to assign top scouts to draft scouting before the draft and not to reassign them afterwards. Scout reports disappear from the draft board if a scout is reassigned before the draft takes place. Assigning scouts to intensive draft scouting from approximately April onward produces the best coverage.

### Round-by-Round Expectations

The first round — particularly the top ten picks — reliably produces top-line and top-pairing talent. The 2007 NHL draft in the default database has a relatively thin second tier; experienced players often prioritise moving up for a specific target rather than holding multiple mid-round picks in that draft.

The second round produces regular NHL contributors and the occasional star, particularly as regens begin entering the pool from the 2009 draft onward. Third-round picks and beyond are genuinely uncertain but can yield NHL regulars — the community's own tracking data showed roughly 40% of picks from round three onward developing into NHL-level players in longer saves.

One community member tracked every pick they made from round three onward across a save running to the 2031–32 season, using consistent criteria for what counted as an "NHL regular" (essentially, a player who spent the majority of their career at NHL level and played close to 900+ NHL games). The results by round:

| Round | NHL regulars | Stars / franchise players | Starting goalies |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3rd | ~50% of picks | 3 | 2 |
| 4th | ~27% of picks | 1 | 1 |
| 5th | ~32% of picks | 3 | 0 |
| 6th | ~55% of picks | 4 | 0 |
| 7th | ~41% of picks | 2 | 2 |

The surprising success rate in rounds six and seven reflects the value of drafting players with strong mental characteristics and high hidden potential who are simply overlooked by AI teams and community scouting reports alike. It also reflects how regens — who begin entering the pool from roughly the 2009 draft onward — can fall to late rounds if their early-career statistics do not impress the in-game scouting systems.

Specific community finds from later rounds include:

- **Overagers** drafted in rounds four through seven can deliver NHL value more quickly than younger players. A 20- or 21-year-old taken in round five may be AHL-ready immediately and on an NHL roster within two years.
- European players drafted in mid-to-late rounds are frequently underrated by AI scouts because of the difficulty in evaluating overseas leagues. Sending scouts specifically to the KHL, SHL, Liiga, and DEL turns up useful late-round talent.
- Regens — players regenerated from retired NHL stars — can occasionally fall into late rounds due to poor mental attributes or weak early seasons. When spotted, they can be exceptional value. Common identifiers include unusual physical profiles (height/weight matching a known former player) or a Flair rating significantly higher than other attributes.

### Scouting Strategy for Later Rounds

The most effective approach for finding value outside the first round is comprehensive geographic scouting combined with specific attribute filtering at the draft itself.

**Scout deployment:** Sending multiple scouts (ten or more if possible) to the major hockey nations and leagues well before the draft produces the deepest prospect coverage. The recommended split is several scouts to the main Canadian junior leagues (OHL, WHL, QMJHL), several to the major European leagues (KHL, SHL, DEL, Liiga, Czech Extraliga), and one or two to less traditional markets — including Britain, Denmark, smaller European leagues, and even non-hockey countries. The last category occasionally yields a genuinely surprising prospect, and the cost of sending a scout there is low. Scouts assigned to regional scouting in the 15–21 age range cover the most relevant prospects.

Scouts should be set to intensive draft scouting from approximately April 1 onward, and must not be reassigned to any other task before the draft. Reassigning a scout causes their reports to disappear from the draft board.

**At the draft itself:** Rather than relying solely on star ratings and ISS rankings, sorting the available player pool by individual attributes produces a different picture. Sorting by attributes that matter for a specific position — Wristshot and Passing for a scoring forward, Pokecheck and Positioning for a defensive defenceman — can surface players who do not appear in the top rankings but have genuine NHL tools. Unranked players from outside North America are particularly worth examining this way, as the AI tends to undervalue them.

**When no scouting report exists:** Players who appear in the draft pool without having been scouted can still be assessed by visible attributes. A player with reasonable technical numbers, good physical attributes, and strong mental characteristics (Determination, Ambition, Strong Character in the report card) is worth a late pick even without scout coverage. These players occasionally turn out to be outstanding.

### Draft Depth by Era

The depth and quality of the draft pool changes meaningfully over the course of a long save. The earliest drafts (2007–2009 in EHM 2007) are composed mainly of real players already in the database; quality in later rounds is limited. From around the 2009 draft onward, regens begin filling the pool, and the overall depth of the draft increases noticeably. By the time a save reaches the mid-2010s in game time, the regens and newgens (EHM:EA) populating the draft are more numerous and the later rounds become significantly more productive.

Some community members report cycles of good and bad draft years even within the regen era — stretches where strong prospects are plentiful, followed by periods where even second-round picks look thin. This is believed to reflect the timing of retirements and regeneration cycles rather than a game flaw.

The practical implication is that early saves are better suited to stockpiling picks for the 2010+ drafts if possible. Trading first and second-round picks in the 2007–2008 window for proven NHL players is a more viable strategy than hoping those picks return star players.

### Mentals, Physicals, and Player Size

There is consistent community debate about whether player size (height and weight) directly influences in-game performance. The developer position, based on forum discussion, is that raw height and weight do not independently affect the game engine — only the physical attributes derived from them (Strength, Speed, Acceleration, Balance) matter. However, several users report that very small players — those under 5'10" or 175 lbs — seem to receive lower average ratings than their attributes alone would predict, and that larger, more physical players rate above expectations in defensive or physical roles.

The most reliable guidance is to treat physical attributes as the decisive factors, not the listed height and weight. However, given anecdotal evidence about smaller players underperforming, most experienced community members avoid drafting very small players in high rounds unless their attribute profile is exceptional.

Heavy, physical players (high Strength, Hitting, Aggression) in defensive or enforcer roles frequently rate higher than scouts predict. A fourth-line enforcer with 20 Strength and 19 Aggression can produce average ratings of 7.5–8.0 from limited ice time purely through physical contributions. These players are a useful source of undervalued late picks and waiver claims.

### The TBL Database and Starting Year UFAs

When using the TBL updated database, a number of players will be free agents at the start of the game who were under contract in the default database, reflecting more recent real-world situations. These players are identified as available and popular targets because the AI teams pursue them actively. Having a high-reputation team or acting quickly on July 1 improves the chance of signing these players over AI competition.

## Player Development Advice

### Junior Players (CHL)

Players drafted out of Canadian Major Junior leagues (OHL, WHL, QMJHL) are subject to age restrictions: 18- and 19-year-olds must return to juniors or play at NHL level — they cannot be assigned to the AHL or ECHL. At 20 they become eligible for the AHL; at 21 their junior eligibility expires.

The consensus recommendation is to leave CHL prospects in juniors until they are averaging at least 8.5 in their current league, or until they have exhausted their junior eligibility. Bringing a 19-year-old up for minimal ice time on the fourth line generally stunts development. Leaving a player in juniors with coaches who work well with youngsters (look for a "Working with Youngsters" attribute of 15 or higher) tends to produce better long-term results.

Sending a signed CHL prospect *back* to their junior team after an NHL signing can sometimes cause attribute regression. This appears to be a known quirk; if a player's attributes drop sharply after being returned to juniors, it is likely tied to the quality of the junior coaching staff rather than a fundamental development issue.

### European Prospects

European prospects left in their home leagues develop at the pace dictated by their coaches and ice time. If a prospect is receiving good playing time with a well-coached team in a strong league, there is generally no need to rush them over. Signing them to an NHL contract and assigning them to the AHL is the alternative, and a year or two in the AHL under controlled development conditions often accelerates progress.

Players left in weaker European leagues or with bad coaching tend to stagnate. The general approach is: if the league is strong and the coaches are rated well, let them develop in Europe. If the league is obscure or the coaches are poor, sign them and bring them to North America.

### Goalies

Goalies are the position the community most consistently identifies as the hardest to develop. The standard recommendation is at least two full AHL seasons before a first NHL start, with good goaltending coaches (strong ratings in Coaching Goalies and Working with Youngsters). College-based goalie development is widely regarded as unreliable because NCAA coaching staff tends to be weak in the game.

For top goalie prospects, common advice is: start in the AHL as a backup for one season, move to starter the following season, then call up to NHL backup duty in season three. Exceptional talents can be moved faster, but patience is generally rewarded.

Key goalie attributes to prioritise: Reflexes, Positioning, Glove, Blocker, Anticipation, and Agility. Passing and Stick Handling matter less unless you use the goalie actively in play.

### College Players

College coaching in the game is notoriously poor. Coaches at NCAA programs frequently lack meaningful attributes in coaching forwards, defensemen, or working with youngsters. The practical result is that players left in college develop slowly or not at all. If a player has CHL rights available, returning them to juniors is often better for development than leaving them in the NCAA. If no CHL rights exist, signing them directly and assigning them to the AHL is the recommended approach.
